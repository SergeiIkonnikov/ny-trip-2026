A garden inside an airport

The green zone at Hamad airport is called The Orchard: a tropical garden of about 10,000 square metres under a glass roof, opened in November 2023.

And in the middle of the main hall sits a seven-metre yellow teddy bear with a lamp on its head. It's by the Swiss artist Urs Fischer. It sold at Christie's for 6.8 million dollars.

The sky the wrong way round

The ceiling of Grand Central's main hall is painted with constellations. Look closely and the sky is mirrored, back to front. The mistake was noticed soon after the station opened in 1913. The explanation offered was a nice one: it's the sky as God sees it, from above.

In one corner a small dark rectangle has been left uncleaned. That's what the whole ceiling looked like before the 1990s restoration: black from decades of tobacco smoke.

The bull nobody ordered

Nobody ordered the bronze bull. The sculptor Arturo Di Modica cast it with his own money and, one night in December 1989, without any permission, trucked it in and set it down in front of the New York Stock Exchange. A Christmas present to the city. The bull weighs over three tonnes.

The police hauled it away, the public objected, and the bull was put up nearby, where it still stands.

Fearless Girl was installed in 2017, facing the bull. A year later she was moved to the Stock Exchange. Now she stares at the Exchange, and the bull stares at nothing.

A plane in the skyscraper

The Empire State Building went up in just over a year and opened in 1931.

In July 1945, in thick fog, a B-25 bomber flew into it around the 79th floor. The elevator operator Betty Lou Oliver fell 75 storeys in her elevator car and survived. It's still the record for the longest survived elevator fall. Less than 48 hours later, many floors were back at work.

The waterfall they switched off

In 1969 the American Falls were switched off. US Army engineers built a temporary dam and from June to November there were no falls: the water was diverted so they could study the rock and see whether it might collapse. By December the water was flowing again. In the end they decided to leave the pile of rocks at the base alone.

A square named after a newspaper

Until 1904 Times Square was called Longacre Square, a place for the horse and carriage trade. Then The New York Times moved in, and the square was renamed after it. The New Year's Eve ball has dropped here since 1907.

The woman who finished the bridge

The Brooklyn Bridge was designed by John Roebling, who died before construction even began. His son Washington took over, and then he too was struck down by caisson disease from working under the river. From then on he watched the construction through a telescope from his bedroom.

Every instruction to the site went through his wife, Emily Roebling. She taught herself the maths, the strength of materials and bridge engineering, and for eleven years effectively ran the project. When the bridge opened in 1883 she was the first to ride across it, holding a rooster, a symbol of victory.

The teacher in the barrel

The first person to go over Niagara Falls in a barrel and survive was a schoolteacher, Annie Edson Taylor. It was 1901, on her 63rd birthday. She hoped for fame and fortune. She got the fame, not the fortune. Climbing out of the barrel, she said nobody should ever try it again.

The guard that never leaves

At the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier at Arlington, an honour guard has stood without a break since 1937: day and night, through heat, snow and hurricanes. The sentinel takes exactly 21 steps along the tomb, turns, waits 21 seconds and walks back. 21, like the 21-gun salute, the highest military honour.

The sign above the table

Katz's has been open since 1888. During the Second World War their slogan was "Send a salami to your boy in the army," and they really did ship salami to soldiers overseas.

Above one table hangs a sign: "Where Harry met Sally... hope you have what she had!" That's the table where the famous scene from the 1989 film was shot.

The Ghostbusters firehouse

It isn't a set. The building at 14 North Moore Street in Tribeca went up in 1903 and has housed a working fire company, Hook & Ladder 8, ever since. The 1984 film used only the outside. The interior scenes were shot in Los Angeles, in an old fire station there.

In 2011 the city planned to close the company to save money. New Yorkers fought for it and won. The ghost sign that now hangs over the doors was made and donated by fans of the film from Buffalo.

Hydrants and Siamese twins

New York has about a hundred thousand fire hydrants. In summer people open them instead of fountains, and have done so since at least the late 19th century. For years the city fought this with fines, then gave in and found a compromise: any firehouse will give you a special spray cap. The water flows, kids are happy, and there's still enough pressure left for fires.

The hydrant is usually credited to Frederick Graff of Philadelphia, in 1801. But nobody can prove it: in 1836 the US Patent Office burned down along with most of its records. The fire hydrant patent may have been lost in a fire.

And this brass thing with two yellow caps on an office building's wall isn't a hydrant but its opposite. Firefighters pump water through it into the building's pipes and sprinklers. Because of its two inlets, it's called a Siamese connection.

Twenty-one elephants

When the Brooklyn Bridge opened, plenty of people doubted it would hold. A year later, in 1884, P. T. Barnum's circus marched 21 elephants across it, led by the famous Jumbo. The bridge held. The circus got publicity, the city got peace of mind.

A portal on the Brooklyn Bridge

In Kate & Leopold (2001) the Brooklyn Bridge works as a time machine. The physicist Stuart (Liev Schreiber) finds a portal under it and falls into 1876. There Duke Leopold (Hugh Jackman) chases him, and they both tumble into the present, into Meg Ryan's life. In the finale it's Kate who jumps off the bridge to go back to Leopold in the 19th century.

In 1876 the bridge was still being built, and in the film Leopold sketches it at a ceremony marking the completion of the Manhattan tower. Among the characters at that ceremony is John Roebling. In real history, as the note about Emily Roebling says, he died in 1869, before construction even began. But in a film with a time machine, anything goes.

That view from DUMBO

Everyone who makes it to DUMBO takes this shot: the corner of Washington and Water Streets, the Manhattan Bridge between two brick warehouses, and under its arch, if you get the angle right, the Empire State Building. We got it right. The bridge, by the way, isn't the Brooklyn Bridge but its neighbour, the Manhattan Bridge, opened in 1909, 26 years after the Brooklyn.

Sergio Leone made this view famous. Several scenes of Once Upon a Time in America (1984) were filmed here, and the shot of the bridge at the end of the street went onto the poster. Ever since, it's been one of the most recognisable streets in New York. And a few steps away, in the archway under the same bridge, Joaquin Phoenix's Joker runs through the 2019 film's trailer.

The name DUMBO itself stands for Down Under the Manhattan Bridge Overpass. Locals coined it in 1978, hoping such an unattractive name would scare off developers. It didn't.

Plus Deposit

Coca-Cola in a glass bottle, $2.29. Under the price, in small print: "plus deposit". Since 1982 New York State has charged a five-cent deposit on every bottle and can, refunded when you return the container. Hence the people outside supermarkets with enormous bags of cans.

A split second made immortal

A sign at the Marine Corps War Memorial in Washington. The memorial reproduces Joe Rosenthal's famous photo: six Marines raising the flag on Iwo Jima in February 1945. Few people know the flag went up twice that day, and the photo shows the second time: the first flag was swapped for a bigger one. Rosenthal won the Pulitzer Prize for it.

→ Day 7 · 10 Nov

A FedEx truck and steam from underground

An ordinary Midtown picture: a FedEx truck, and behind it steam pouring up from under the asphalt. There are two New York stories in this one shot.

The first is the steam. It isn't an accident. Since 1882 a steam network has run under Manhattan, carrying steam through pipes under the streets into buildings to heat them. Today it's run by the utility Con Edison, more than 1,700 buildings are connected, and it is the largest commercial steam system in the world, bigger than the next nine combined. The steam above the street comes from leaks or condensation and is vented upwards through ten-foot orange-and-white stacks.

The second is the logo. Look at the gap between the E and the x: there's a white arrow pointing forward. The designer Lindon Leader drew it in 1994. Once you've seen it, you can't unsee it.
